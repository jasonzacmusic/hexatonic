## 1. 🔴 THE SIX CORRECTIONS — read these first

### 1.1 "Gospel scale" does NOT mean our no-4 hexatonic. This was my worst error.

**"Gospel scale" overwhelmingly means `1 2 b3 3 5 6` — the major blues scale.**
Piano With Jonny (Jonny May) teaches the Gospel Scale as **C D Eb E G A**, aliases
*Major Blues* and *Pentatonic♭3*, with two courses named for it. Grounded further in
Dan Greenblatt, *The Blues Scales* (Sher Music).

I had labelled our `1 2 3 5 6 7` collection "Sunday / gospel" throughout. **That is
wrong and it is the highest-risk claim in the whole pack** — a gospel player would
correct it in the first comment. All labels are now changed.

There are at least **four** collections circulating under gospel-adjacent names:

| degrees | on C | name in circulation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 2 b3 3 5 6 | C D Eb E G A | **"Gospel Scale"** / Major Blues |
| 1 2 3 4 5 6 | C D E F G A | "Major Hexatonic"; nicknamed "gospel" by Aledort |
| **1 2 3 5 6 7** | **C D E G A B** | "Major Hexatonic" (Hear & Play only) |
| 1 2 b3 3 5 b6 6 | C D Eb E G Ab A | "Country Blues Scale" |

### 1.2 "Major hexatonic" more commonly means the **no-7**, not the no-4

| meaning | who uses it that way |
|---|---|
| **1 2 3 4 5 6** (no 7th) | Wikipedia *Hexatonic scale*; Jay Skyler ("Hexatonic Major", a.k.a. "Allman Brothers Scale"); Andy Aledort, *Guitar World* |
| **1 2 3 5 6 7** (no 4th) | Hear & Play (Chuku Onyemachi); Josiah Boornazian, Learn Jazz Standards |

**Never ship a bare "major hexatonic" label.** Always qualify it.

### 1.3 ⚠️ "Sunday scale" is a REAL term — but not ours, and its content is unverified

**"The Sunday Scale" is established, and by a serious name: Peter Martin (Open
Studio).** It is Lesson 2 of 8 in *Elements of Gospel Piano*, with its own funnel
(sundayscale.com) and a free YouTube lesson. A user transcription is titled "The
Sunday Scale Hexatonic," so it *is* six notes — **but the actual note content sits
behind a PDF signup and paid course and could not be verified.**

**Do not call our scale the "Sunday scale."** It may well be someone else's
six-note scale, and Peter Martin is not a person to be casually contradicted.

### 1.4 ⚠️ "Before Western music had seven notes, it had six" — DROP THIS

This was my proposed title and hook. **It does not survive scholarly scrutiny.**

**Stefano Mengozzi, *The Renaissance Reform of Medieval Music Theory: Guido of
Arezzo between Myth and History* (Cambridge UP, 2010)** argues that six-syllable
solmization was only ever a **sight-singing option**, and never imposed
"sixth-ness" onto a diatonic system **already grounded on seven letters**. The
hexachord's promotion to a *fundamental diatonic structure* was the work of
humanist-era theorists reading it back onto Guido.

So: **do not say Guido "invented the hexachord system," and do not say music had
six notes before seven.** What is safe:

> *"For centuries, Western musicians learned to sing using a six-note unit — Guido
> d'Arezzo's ut re mi fa sol la. The notes we're about to play are exactly that
> hexachord."*

That is true, sourced, and still a great hook. Two further caveats: the **Guidonian
hand almost certainly post-dates Guido** (the diagram appears only in 12th-century
manuscripts), and *Micrologus* is c. 1026, *Epistola* c. 1030s, Guido c. 991–after
1033.

### 1.5 The avoid-note doctrine is HARMONIC, not melodic — and the minor case is contested

Every source agrees: an avoid note is **barred from voicings and sustained melody
notes**, but remains fully available as an approach, passing or neighbour tone.
Saying "you can't play the 4th" is a misstatement of the doctrine.

- **4th/11th over Imaj7 — bulletproof.** F sits a half step above E, the 3rd of
  Cmaj7. Confirmed in Nettles & Graf, Wikipedia, PianoGroove, Hear & Play, Terefenko.
- **b6 over i-7 (Aeolian) — attested but genuinely CONTESTED.** Nettles & Graf list
  Aeolian → avoid note 6; **PianoGroove explicitly does not**, treating b6 as
  Aeolian's characteristic note. There is a further live split on Dorian's 6th.

**Consequence for Theorem 3:** the claim "the avoid note is exactly the note we
removed" is **solid for the major** and **arguable for the minor**. Present the
minor case as *"many teachers treat the b6 as the avoid note over i-7; some
disagree and hear it as Aeolian's defining colour."* The **tritone** argument
(Theorem 1) is unaffected — that one is arithmetic and holds either way. **Lead
with the tritone, not the avoid note.**

### 1.6 Barry Harris — there are FOUR scales, not three, and the dominant one uses Ab

I wrote "major/minor/dominant." There are **four**, each a chord interlocked with
the diminished 7th on its **major-7th degree**:

| Barry's name | chord | scale on C |
|---|---|---|
| Major Sixth Diminished | C6 | **C D E F G Ab A B** |
| Minor Sixth Diminished | Cm6 | **C D Eb F G Ab A B** |
| Seventh Diminished | C7 | **C D E F G Ab Bb B** |
| Seventh Flat Five Diminished | C7b5 | **C D E F Gb Ab Bb B** |

**The dominant scale is `C D E F G Ab Bb B` — with Ab.** `C D E F G A Bb B` is the
**bebop dominant scale** and is *not* a sixth-diminished scale. Proof: alternate
notes of the Ab version give C7 (C E G Bb) and a true dim7 (D F Ab B); the A version
gives Bm7b5, which is half-diminished and cannot interleave.

Three more traps:
- **There is no "major 7th diminished scale."** Kingstone uses that phrase for a
  **chord** (the VII° diminished).
- **Never call these "octatonic."** They are eight-note but provably *not* the
  symmetric diminished scale — that one has 3 transpositions and interval pattern
  `2 1 2 1 2 1 2 1`; Barry's four all have 12 transpositions. Say "eight-note."
- **"Sixth" refers purely to the sixth CHORD. There is no six-note collection
  anywhere in Barry's system.** This is exactly why his method keeps getting
  mis-filed under hexatonics — kill that confusion on sight.

## 2. The naming system we should actually use — modal intersection

This is the fix for §1.1 and §1.2, and it is **better than what I had**, because it
is both sourced and self-explaining.

Order the modes on a common tonic by brightness — Lydian, Ionian, Mixolydian,
Dorian, Aeolian, Phrygian, Locrian. Adjacent modes differ by exactly one note, and
**their intersection is always a 6-32 hexachord.** All six:

| modal pair | intersection on C | degrees |
|---|---|---|
| **Ionian / Lydian** | **C D E G A B** | **1 2 3 5 6 7** ← our no-4 |
| **Ionian / Mixolydian** | **C D E F G A** | **1 2 3 4 5 6** ← our no-7 |
| Dorian / Mixolydian | C D F G A Bb | 1 2 4 5 6 b7 |
| **Dorian / Aeolian** | **C D Eb F G Bb** | **1 2 b3 4 5 b7** ← our minor |
| Phrygian / Aeolian | C Eb F G Ab Bb | 1 b3 4 5 b6 b7 |
| Phrygian / Locrian | C Db Eb F Ab Bb | 1 b2 b3 4 b6 b7 |

So our no-4 collection is **not** "Ionian hexatonic" — it is the **Ionian/Lydian
hexatonic**, the collection that refuses to commit to 4 or #4. *That is exactly why
jazz players use it over both Cmaj7 and Cmaj7#11.* And the "Dorian ambiguity" I
identified in the minor hexatonic is precisely nameable: it is the **Dorian/Aeolian
hexatonic**, which removes the very note distinguishing those two modes.

**This is not an invention — it is the established folk-song analysis system.**
Cecil Sharp classified Appalachian tunes exactly this way:

| Sharp type | missing degree | label |
|---|---|---|
| 1b Hexatonic | 7th | **Ionian or Mixolydian** |
| 2b Hexatonic | 6th | **Dorian or Aeolian** |
| 4a Hexatonic | 3rd | Mixolydian or Dorian |
| 5a Hexatonic | 2nd | Aeolian or Phrygian |

Lineage continues with **Bertrand Harris Bronson, "Folksong and the Modes,"
*Musical Quarterly* (1946)**, "Are the Modes Outmoded?" (1972), and *The
Traditional Tunes of the Child Ballads* (Princeton UP).

### The labels to ship

| degrees | primary label | secondary |
|---|---|---|
| 1 2 3 5 6 7 | **Ionian/Lydian Hexatonic** | "major hexatonic (no 4)" |
| 1 2 3 4 5 6 | **Ionian/Mixolydian Hexatonic** | "Guidonian hexachord" |
| 1 2 b3 4 5 b7 | **Dorian/Aeolian Hexatonic** | "minor hexatonic (no b6)" |

⚠️ **UNVERIFIED:** Samuel Bayard's role in hexatonic naming; and "major hexatonic"
as standard **bluegrass/country curriculum** vocabulary — it is attested in folk-song
*scholarship* and guitar pedagogy, not confirmed as fiddle/banjo teaching language.
Do not claim it.

## 3. Bonus provable facts worth building features around

**(i) 6-32 is one of only FIVE tritone-free hexachord set classes** out of 50.
Exhaustive enumeration of all 924 six-note subsets gives: (012345), (013458),
**(014589) = 6-20 augmented**, (023457), and **(024579) = 6-32**. So our two
headline families are in a five-member club.

**(ii) `G + Am` is the UNIQUE triad pair anywhere that produces C D E G A B.**
Confirmed by exhaustive search over all maj/min/aug/dim triads in all 12 keys.
(One non-triad option: Gsus4 + Esus4.) **Strong, provable app feature.**

**(iii) The general triad-pair rule:** within any 7-note scale, the diatonic triads
with no common tones are exactly those on **adjacent** degrees, and each such pair
yields the hexatonic omitting exactly one scale note — 7 pairs, 7 omissions. In C:
`C+Dm` omits B · `C+B°` omits A · `Dm+Em` omits C · `Em+F` omits D · `F+G` omits E ·
**`G+Am` omits F** · `Am+B°` omits G.

**(iv) Riemann's decomposition** (*Folkloristische Tonalitätsstudien*, 1916, p. 39):
the hexachord = three overlapping tetrachords **and two overlapping pentatonics**.
Verified: {C D E F G A} contains exactly two anhemitonic pentatonic subsets —
C D E G A and C D F G A. No others.

## 4. The word "hexatonic" has four different scopes — disambiguate every time

- **jazz usage:** "the hexatonic scale" usually means the **augmented scale (6-20)**
- **Cohn's usage:** "hexatonic collection" also means 6-20
- **triad-pair pedagogy:** "a hexatonic" = any two-triad six-note set
- **folk-song analysis:** "hexatonic" = any gapped six-note scale

Our app uses the fourth sense primarily and the third in the v2 triad-pair module.
**Say which one you mean on first use in every module.**

## 5. The augmented scale — fully verified (v1 content)

**C D# E G G# B** (= C Eb E G Ab B). Pattern **m3–H–m3–H–m3–H**.
Set class **6-20**, prime (014589), vector **`<303630>`**, **exactly 4
transpositions**. Also the **"Ode-to-Napoleon" hexachord** (Schoenberg Op. 41, 1942)
and the "magic hexachord."

Chord content, computed exhaustively:

| type | roots | count |
|---|---|---|
| major triads | C, E, Ab | **3** |
| minor triads | C, E, Ab | **3** |
| augmented triads | C+ and Eb+ | 2 distinct |
| maj7 / minMaj7 / maj7#5 | C, E, Ab | 3 each |
| **dominant 7ths** | — | **0** |
| **minor 7ths** | — | **0** |
| diminished triads | — | 0 |

**The zero-dominant-7ths result is the teaching point** — it is why this collection
behaves as a *tonic-major* colour, not a dominant one. The three maj7 chords a major
third apart are the three tonic chords of a Giant Steps cycle.

**Players — verified:** **Oliver Nelson's solo on "Stolen Moments"** (*The Blues and
the Abstract Truth*, Impulse!, 1961) is *the* canonical jazz example — cited to Josh
Workman, *Guitar Player* 41/7 (July 2007), p. 108. Coltrane and Nelson, late
1950s–early 60s, same source. Liszt, *A Faust Symphony*, is the celebrated early
appearance. Scholarly bridge to Cohn: **Rich Pellegrin, "Motive, Collection, and
Voice Leading in John Coltrane's 'Giant Steps'," *Jazz Perspectives*** — identifies
a hexatonic core, **Hex(2,3)**, at the centre of "Giant Steps."

⚠️ **Do NOT ship:** Michael Brecker and Woody Shaw as augmented-scale attributions
(widely repeated, no documented solo or analysis found). Shostakovich Piano Trio
No. 2 finale (Wikipedia marks it *citation needed*). Monk/Beiderbecke whole-tone
claims are uncited on Wikipedia.

## 8. Cohn's hexatonic systems (roadmap, v3)

**Richard Cohn, "Maximally Smooth Cycles, Hexatonic Systems, and the Analysis of
Late-Romantic Triadic Progressions," *Music Analysis* 15/1 (1996): 9–40** — SMT
Outstanding Publication Award 1997. Also *Audacious Euphony* (OUP, 2nd ed. 2012)
and "Uncanny Resemblances," *JAMS* 57/2 (2004).

**P** swaps triad quality holding the fifth; **L** maps C major ↔ E minor. Both are
single-semitone moves. Alternating them gives a closed **six-chord PL cycle** with
**3 major + 3 minor triads**, and there are exactly **four** such cycles — because
each cycle's pitch content is one transposition of **6-20**, which has exactly four.

| system | PL cycle | content | Straus label |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | C→Cm→Ab→Abm→E→Em | {C,Eb,E,G,Ab,B} | Hex(3,4) |
| 2 | C#→C#m→A→Am→F→Fm | {C,C#,E,F,Ab,A} | Hex(0,1) |
| 3 | D→Dm→Bb→Bbm→F#→F#m | {C#,D,F,F#,A,Bb} | Hex(1,2) |
| 4 | Eb→Ebm→B→Bm→G→Gm | {D,Eb,F#,G,Bb,B} | **Hex(2,3) = Cohn's WESTERN** |

**Hexatonic poles:** within a system, each triad has exactly one opposite-quality
partner that is *not* a semitone move — it shares **no common tone** and the two
together **exhaust the six notes**. All three voices move by one semitone in the same
direction simultaneously. Cohn's semantic claim: late-Romantic composers used this
to depict **the uncanny** (Wagner *Parsifal*, Prokofiev *Peter and the Wolf*).

⚠️ Only **Western = Hex(2,3)** is directly sourced. Eastern = Hex(0,1) is derivable.
**Northern vs Southern could not be determined** — do not assign them.
⚠️ No specific Brahms example could be confirmed.
